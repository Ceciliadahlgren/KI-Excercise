Premium = (total expected medical claims × 1.10 + fixed administrative costs) ÷ number of insured people. No profit margin.

Debrief: What caused the collapse?

Were the groups acting rationally? Why did the premium rise although nobody's health got worse? Why might a market with medical expenses covered by premiums still disappear? How could regulation change the outcome?

Policy experiment:

The counterfactual removes both fixed and variable administrative costs. The groups and their willingness to pay stay the same. It resets the game so students can test their own decisions again.

Teacher guide + printable confidential group cards

Setup (20–25 minutes). Divide the classroom into four groups; each represents the specified number of identical customers, not the number of students at the table. Give each group its own confidential card. Do not show the others' willingness-to-pay limits. Start with everyone insured, then let groups vote to renew or leave. No re-entry after leaving.

Suggested facilitator script: “You must all pay the same premium. Decide whether insurance is worth buying for your group. You are trying to make the best decision for your own group, not to save the insurer.”

Pricing: Expected medical claims per member differ by risk group. The insurer charges exactly enough to cover expected claims plus 10% variable administration and $1,200 fixed administration for the entire insurance pool. The fixed charge is spread across those still enrolled, so departures increase the cost per insured member.

Expected path, if students leave whenever the premium exceeds willingness to pay: Start ~$470 (22 people) → A leaves: ~$769 (12 people) → B leaves: ~$1,153 (6 people) → C leaves: $1,920 (2 people) → D leaves: market closes. Without administration: ~$377 → ~$608 → ~$867, with C and D willing to stay. Other student decisions produce other outcomes.

Important nuance: Risk groups and willingness to pay are fixed in this stylized model. The insurer breaks even in expectation while operating. “Market collapse” means no group is willing to renew at the posted premium; it does not mean the insurer accumulates losses. Do not claim this trajectory is inevitable in real-world markets.
